There are three main ways to understand tautologies, depending on the context:
**1. In Formal Logic (Philosophy & Mathematics)**
In logic, a tautology is a formula or assertion that is **true in every possible interpretation**. It is unconditionally true, regardless of the truth values of its individual parts.
- **Example:** *"It is raining OR it is not raining."* (Symbolically: **P ∨ ¬P**).

**2. In Rhetoric and Everyday Language (Redundancy)**
In common speech, a tautology is a phrase that says the same thing twice in different words. It is often considered a stylistic flaw because it adds no new information.
- **Examples:**
- *"Free gift"* (a gift is by definition free).
- *"ATM machine"* (the M stands for "Machine").
- *"First priority"* (a priority is inherently first).
- *"It is what it is."* (A circular statement that offers no explanation).
**3. In Computer Science**
In programming and Boolean logic, a tautology refers to a logical condition that always evaluates to `true`. This is often a bug (e.g., an `if` statement that can never be false) or, contrarily, a way to create an infinite loop.
---
**Key Distinction:** A tautology is different from a **contradiction**.
A contradiction (e.g., *"It is raining AND it is not raining"*) is always **false**, whereas a tautology is always **true**."
